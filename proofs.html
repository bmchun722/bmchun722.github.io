Three Proofs Behind a Rate Law, Seen in Soap, Waves and Water

Yoon and Bazant's quantum theory of the metal dependence of electrocatalysis rests on three classical moves: an equilibrium argument, the intersection of two straight lines, and an expansion to the first term that does not vanish. Each move also explains a pattern from Philip Ball's Shapes. Here each proof is worked first on the pattern, where it can be seen, and then on the electrode, using the heuristics of Pólya's How to Solve It.

Proof 1 · Equilibrium

Whatever can move, moves until the forces balance

Auxiliary problemVariation of the problem

The pattern: three soap films at 120°

Ball recounts Plateau's rule: where soap films meet, exactly three walls join, at 120°. Why 120° and not another angle? Make the question concrete. Three films are pinned to three points \(A, B, C\) and join at a free junction \(P\). Surface tension is the same in every film, so the energy is proportional to the total length

$$L(P) = |P - A| + |P - B| + |P - C|.$$
  1. Name the free variable. Only \(P\) can move; the anchors are the data.
  2. Equilibrium means stationarity. \(\nabla L = \hat u_A + \hat u_B + \hat u_C = 0\), where \(\hat u_i\) is the unit vector from \(A_i\) to \(P\). Each film pulls with the same tension, so the gradient is the net force.
  3. Read off the geometry. Three unit vectors that add to zero form a closed equilateral triangle, so each pair is 120° apart, whatever the positions of the anchors, as long as no angle of \(ABC\) reaches 120°.

Drag the anchors or the junction, then press Relax: the junction slides down the gradient until the three pulls cancel.

The electrode: the Fermi energy drops out of equilibrium

Auxiliary problem. Yoon and Bazant do not attack "how does the metal change the rate?" directly. They first solve an easier problem: two inert electrodes, Pt and a metal M, in the same solution at equilibrium, with no net reaction (their Fig. 1a).
Variation of the problem. Change only one datum, the metal, and ask what has to change with it.
  1. Free variable. Electrons can flow between electrode and solution, so the electrode's Galvani potential \(\phi_e\) is free. It plays the role of the junction \(P\).
  2. Stationarity. At equilibrium the reaction free energy vanishes, \(\mu_R - \mu_O - \mu_e = 0\). The redox couple fixes \(\mu_O\) and \(\mu_R\), so it fixes \(\mu_e = \mu_R - \mu_O\). These are the anchors.
  3. Read off the compensation. Since \(\mu_e = \varepsilon_F - e\phi_e\), changing the metal by \(\Delta\varepsilon_F\) must be met by \(e\,\Delta\phi_e = \Delta\varepsilon_F\) (Eq. 8). The equilibrium potential \(E_{eq}\) does not depend on the metal (Eqs. 9–10), just as 120° does not depend on where the anchors are.
Energy levels of Pt (reference) and metal M
\(e\,\Delta\phi_e\): – \(\Delta\mu_e = \Delta\varepsilon_F - e\Delta\phi_e\): – \(E_{eq}\) shift: 0 V
Why the proof matters for kinetics. Thermodynamics cannot see the metal, yet rates do. Yoon and Bazant's hypothesis is that the compensating electrostatic shift \(\Delta\varepsilon_F = e\Delta\phi_e\), together with the surface potential \(\Delta\chi_r\) of the reaction plane, enters the excess energy of the reactive electron (Eqs. 11–12). Proof 2 shows how much of that shift reaches the barrier.
Proof 2 · Two Lines

Where two straight lines cross, a shift is shared in proportion to the slopes

AnalogyFiguresGeneralization

The pattern: territories of chemical waves

In the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction each pacemaker sends out waves. Ball notes that two waves annihilate when they collide, because neither can excite the exhausted medium behind the other, and the collisions draw fixed boundaries between territories. Where does the boundary sit? As a toy model, take one pair of fronts.

Figures. Draw position \(x\) against time \(t\). A front moving at constant speed is a straight line; its slope \(s = 1/v\) is the time needed per unit distance (the slowness).

Source A at \(x = 0\) fires at \(t = 0\); source B at \(x = 1\) fires after a delay \(d\). The fronts are \(t = s_A x\) and \(t = d + s_B (1 - x)\). Setting them equal gives

$$x^{*} = \frac{s_B + d}{s_A + s_B}, \qquad t^{*} = \alpha\,(s_B + d), \qquad \alpha = \frac{s_A}{s_A + s_B}.$$

Delaying B by \(\delta\) delays the collision by only \(\alpha\,\delta\), and moves the boundary towards B by \(\delta/(s_A + s_B)\): A gains territory while B is late. The share \(\alpha\) is set by the slopes alone.

The electrode: the symmetry factor

Analogy. In the ICET regime, CIET treats the ion-transfer landscape as linear near the transition state (following Gurney and Butler). The reactant free energy rises along the ion coordinate \(\xi\) with slope \(\beta_{red}\); the product free energy, offset by the driving force \(\Delta G^{ex}\), rises the other way with slope \(\beta_{ox}\). These are the same two lines.
Generalization. The symmetric case \(s_A = s_B\) gives \(\alpha = 1/2\); the general case gives any \(\alpha\) between 0 and 1.
$$G_O(\xi) = \beta_{red}\,\xi, \qquad G_R(\xi) = \Delta G^{ex} + \beta_{ox}(1-\xi) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{red} = \alpha\,\Delta G^{ex} + \underbrace{\alpha(1-\alpha)(\beta_{red}+\beta_{ox})}_{\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ICET}}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\beta_{red}}{\beta_{red}+\beta_{ox}}$$

The symmetry factor is exactly Eq. 19 of the paper, and the driving-force terms are Eqs. 20–21. The oxidation barrier is \(\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ox} = \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{red} - \Delta G^{ex} = -(1-\alpha)\,\Delta G^{ex} + \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ICET}\), so the net rate is Butler–Volmer with cathodic coefficient \(\alpha\) and anodic coefficient \(1-\alpha\) (Eq. 13). The intrinsic barrier above is that of two bare lines. The paper's Eq. 22, \(\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ICET} = \alpha(1-\alpha)(\beta_{red}+\beta_{ox}+\lambda) - \Delta_e\), also includes the reorganization energy \(\lambda\) and the chemisorption function \(\Delta_e\), which the two-line picture leaves out.

Now combine with Proof 1. The paper writes \(\Delta G^{ex} = \mu^{ex}_R - \mu^{ex}_O - \mu^{ex}_e\), and the metal raises the electron's excess energy \(\mu^{ex}_e\) by \(S = \Delta\varepsilon_F - e\Delta\chi_r\) (Eq. 11). Shifting \(\Delta G^{ex}\) by \(-S\) moves the two barriers in opposite directions, by the shares the two lines dictate: \(\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{red} \to \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{red} - \alpha S\) and \(\Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ox} \to \Delta G^{\ddagger}_{ox} + (1-\alpha) S\). The exchange-current barrier in Eq. 14 shifts by \((1-\alpha)(\Delta\varepsilon_F - e\Delta\chi_r)\), so the exchange current changes by the factor \(\exp[-(1-\alpha)S/k_BT]\).

The pattern: why a thread of water breaks into beads

Ball describes the Rayleigh instability: dew on a spider thread, or a thin jet of water, breaks into a string of beads. Surface tension minimizes area, but a cylinder already looks smooth. Does a ripple raise or lower the area?

Specialization. Take one ripple of wavenumber \(k\) and small amplitude \(\varepsilon\): \(r(z) = a\,(1 + \varepsilon\cos kz)\), with \(a\) chosen so the volume of liquid stays fixed.
  1. Volume constraint. \(\pi a^2 (1 + \varepsilon^2/2) = \pi R^2\), so \(a \approx R(1 - \varepsilon^2/4)\).
  2. Expand the area. The first-order term averages to zero over a wavelength, so expand to second order: $$\frac{\Delta A}{A_0} = \frac{\varepsilon^2}{4}\,\bigl(k^2R^2 - 1\bigr) + O(\varepsilon^4).$$
  3. Read the sign. Ripples longer than the circumference (\(kR < 1\), wavelength \(> 2\pi R\)) lower the area, so the column breaks up.

The area argument gives only the threshold. Ball stresses that one wavelength grows fastest and sets the bead size; finding it needs the flow inside the thread (Rayleigh's dynamic analysis), not just the area. At large amplitude the threshold also shifts slightly, by terms of order \(\varepsilon^4\), which the panel shows near \(kR = 1\).

Test by dimension. The answer can depend only on the dimensionless product \(kR\). Dimensions alone do not fix the threshold; the expansion supplies the value \(kR = 1\). Can you check the result? The panel integrates the exact area numerically and compares it with the expansion.

The electrode: two limits of one theory

The general CIET rate contains both a Marcus-like electron-transfer part and a Butler–Volmer-like ion-transfer part. Yoon and Bazant obtain the working formulas as limits, compared by the dimensionless ratios of energies:

  • ICET, ion transfer limiting, \(\beta_{red/ox} \gg \lambda,\ e|\eta|\): Butler–Volmer with the symmetry factor of Proof 2 (Eq. 13).
  • ECIT, electron transfer limiting, \(\beta_{red/ox} \ll \lambda\): a Marcus–Hush–Chidsey-type rate (Eq. 23, exchange current Eq. 25).

The same expansion argument shows why Butler–Volmer and Marcus agree near equilibrium and part ways at high overpotential. Take the Marcus barrier for reduction at driving force \(e\eta\) (with the paper's \(\eta = (\mu_R - \mu_O - \mu_e)/e\), Eq. 10), and expand in the dimensionless ratio \(e\eta/\lambda\):

$$\Delta G^{\ddagger} = \frac{(\lambda + e\eta)^2}{4\lambda} = \underbrace{\frac{\lambda}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\,e\eta}_{\text{Butler–Volmer, }\alpha = 1/2} + \underbrace{\frac{(e\eta)^2}{4\lambda}}_{\text{curved Tafel plot}}.$$

The first-order term is Butler–Volmer with \(\alpha = 1/2\). The second-order term has a fixed sign, and it bends the Tafel plot once \(|e\eta|\) is no longer small compared with \(\lambda\). In our reading, this curvature is the practical signature that separates Marcus-like from Butler–Volmer kinetics in experiments.
