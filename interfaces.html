Ions at Interfaces

Three short notes: when mean field electrostatics holds, why a growing electrode roughens, and how competing forces set a size, from an electrode to a liquid column to a micelle.

1 · When Mean Field Holds

Mean field (Poisson–Boltzmann) electrostatics lets each ion feel only the average potential of the others. This is fair when the neighbours are many and weakly coupled: the Bjerrum length \(\ell_B\), where two unit charges interact with \(k_BT\), must be much shorter than the other lengths of the problem. In the bulk, \(\ell_B\) meets the Debye length \(\lambda_D\) and the mean ion spacing \(d\); at the electrode, it meets the Gouy–Chapman length \(\ell_{GC}\) and the counterion spacing \(a\).

$$\ell_B=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon k_BT},\qquad \varepsilon_c=\frac{\ell_B}{\lambda_D}\ \text{(bulk)},\qquad \Xi=\frac{\ell_B}{\ell_{GC}},\ \ \ell_{GC}=\frac{2\varepsilon\varepsilon_0k_BT}{e|\sigma|}\ \text{(charged electrode)}$$ $$d=(2cN_A)^{-1/3}\ \text{(bulk, 1:1 salt)},\qquad a=\sqrt{e/|\sigma|}\ \text{(electrode)}$$
SymbolPhysical meaning
\(k_BT\)thermal energy: the typical energy of random thermal motion per particle, \(4.1\times10^{-21}\) J = 25.7 meV at 298 K. An attraction weaker than \(k_BT\) is shaken apart by thermal motion; a stronger one holds two ions together
\(\ell_B\)distance at which two unit charges in the solvent interact with energy \(k_BT\)
\(\lambda_D\)Debye length: how far the field of one ion reaches before the ion cloud screens it (shorter at higher salt); \(\lambda_D^2=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon k_BT/(2e^2c)\) for a 1:1 salt of concentration \(c\)
\(\ell_{GC}\)Gouy–Chapman length: distance over which a unit charge gains \(k_BT\) in the field of the bare electrode (surface charge \(\sigma\)); in PB, the thickness of the counterion layer (a charged wall in [1])
\(d,\ a\)mean spacing between ions in the bulk, and between counterions on the electrode
\(\varepsilon_c,\ \Xi\)coupling strengths: \(\ell_B\) compared with \(\lambda_D\) (bulk) or \(\ell_{GC}\) (electrode); mean field needs them well below 1

The three lengths drawn to one scale for water (\(\varepsilon=78.4\)) at 298 K, 0.1 M 1:1 salt and an electrode at 30 µC/cm² (my calculation from Eqs. 21 and 22 of [1]); ion sizes not to scale. Counterions on the electrode are \(a=0.73\) nm apart. Whether these sizes break mean field is the subject of “When mean field breaks” below.

What “feeling the average” means

The potential \(\phi\) at an ion depends on where every neighbour is at that moment (left). Mean field replaces it by its ensemble average \(\langle\phi\rangle\), the potential of the average (smeared) charge density \(\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)\), which still varies in space, for example across the double layer (right).

Poisson–Boltzmann combines two exact statements, (A) and (C), with one approximation, (B), which is also used to evaluate the bulk value in (C):

(A) Gauss's law, averaged (exact, uniform \(\varepsilon\)). At any instant the ions are point charges at positions \(\mathbf r_i\). Averaging over all their positions turns the sum of delta functions into the number densities \(\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)\); because Gauss's law is linear, the average passes straight through ([1] Eq. 11, p.7, in Green's function form; differential form Eq. 38, p.13). The electrode's own potential \(\phi_{\text{ext}}\) in Eq. 11 enters below as a boundary condition:

$$-\nabla\cdot\big(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\nabla\phi\big)=\sum_i q_i\,\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i) \quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{average}\ }\quad -\nabla\cdot\big(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\nabla\langle\phi\rangle\big)=q_+\rho_+(\mathbf r)+q_-\rho_-(\mathbf r)\qquad\text{(A)}$$
PieceRead it as
\(\nabla\phi\)slope of the potential in each direction; \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi\) is the electric field
\(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\nabla\phi\)\(\nabla\phi\) times the permittivity \(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\) (how strongly the solvent polarizes); since \(\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi\), this is \(-\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\mathbf E=-\mathbf D\), minus the displacement field
\(\nabla\cdot(\ \cdot\ )\)divergence: how much of that field flows out of a tiny volume around \(\mathbf r\)
whole of (A)field lines leave a point only where there is charge: positive charge is a source, negative a sink
in 1D\(-\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\,d^2\phi/dx^2=\) charge density. \(d^2\phi/dx^2\) is the curvature of \(\phi\): the potential bends where there is charge (positive charge bends it downward, \(\phi''<0\)) and is a straight line where there is none. In 3D, \(\nabla^2\) sums the curvatures along \(x,y,z\); \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) means they cancel

One equation, but three unknowns: \(\langle\phi\rangle,\rho_+,\rho_-\). We need a rule for how the ions arrange themselves.

(B) Mean field chemical potential (the approximation). The cost of adding an ion at \(\mathbf r\) is its ideal gas part plus its charge times the average potential; the electrode energy \(V\), ion size, self energy and correlations between ions are dropped:

$$\mu_\pm(\mathbf r)=k_BT\ln\!\big(\Lambda^3\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)\big)+q_\pm\langle\phi(\mathbf r)\rangle\qquad\text{(B)}$$

(C) Equilibrium (exact). Ions move until adding one costs the same everywhere, so \(\mu_\pm(\mathbf r)=\mu_\pm^b\), its value in the bulk. Evaluating (B) in the bulk, where \(\rho_\pm=\rho_\pm^b\) and \(\langle\phi\rangle=0\), gives \(\mu_\pm^b\):

$$\mu_\pm(\mathbf r)=\mu_\pm^{b}=k_BT\ln\!\big(\Lambda^3\rho_\pm^b\big)\qquad\text{(C)}$$

(B) + (C) give the Boltzmann distribution. Set (B) equal to (C); \(\Lambda^3\) cancels:

$$k_BT\ln\!\big(\Lambda^3\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)\big)+q_\pm\langle\phi(\mathbf r)\rangle=k_BT\ln\!\big(\Lambda^3\rho_\pm^b\big) \quad\Rightarrow\quad k_BT\ln\frac{\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)}{\rho_\pm^b}=-q_\pm\langle\phi(\mathbf r)\rangle$$
$$\rho_\pm(\mathbf r)=\rho_\pm^b\,e^{-q_\pm\langle\phi(\mathbf r)\rangle/k_BT}\qquad\text{(Boltzmann)}$$

Where \(\langle\phi\rangle\) is negative, cations gather and anions are pushed away, each by a Boltzmann factor.

Boltzmann into (A) gives Poisson–Boltzmann. Now only \(\langle\phi\rangle\) is unknown. For a 1:1 salt (\(q_\pm=\pm e\), \(\rho_\pm^b=c\)) next to a flat electrode:

$$-\nabla\cdot\big(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\nabla\langle\phi\rangle\big)=\sum_\pm q_\pm\rho_\pm^b\,e^{-q_\pm\langle\phi\rangle/k_BT}\qquad\text{(PB)}$$ $$\text{1:1 salt, flat electrode:}\quad \varepsilon_0\varepsilon\,\frac{d^2\langle\phi\rangle}{dx^2}=2ec\,\sinh\!\frac{e\langle\phi\rangle}{k_BT}$$

Read (PB) as: the curvature of the average potential is set by the ions that this same potential has gathered (left side from (A), right side from Boltzmann).

The electrode enters as a boundary condition, again Gauss's law at the surface: \(-\varepsilon_0\varepsilon\,d\langle\phi\rangle/dx\,|_{x=0}=\sigma\).

Everything rests on (B): it fails when an ion's neighbours do respond to it, which \(\varepsilon_c\) and \(\Xi\) measure.

When mean field breaks

(B) treats the positions of neighbours as uncorrelated: a neighbour is equally likely to sit anywhere near the ion. A neighbour at spacing \(s\) (\(d\) in the bulk, \(a\) at the electrode) actually sits next to the ion more or less often than at random, by the Boltzmann weight of their bare Coulomb energy (\(g\): how much more or less often than at random; (B) sets \(g=1\)):

$$\frac{U(s)}{k_BT}=\frac{\ell_B}{s},\qquad g=e^{-U/k_BT}=e^{\mp\ell_B/s}\quad(\text{like / opposite charges})$$

So \(\ell_B/s\) is a ruler for the error of (B). The two coupling numbers are this ruler in disguise (my algebra): \(\varepsilon_c=\sqrt{4\pi}\,(\ell_B/d)^{3/2}\) in the bulk and \(\Xi=2\pi(\ell_B/a)^2\) at the electrode. \(\varepsilon_c\) or \(\Xi\approx1\) means \(\ell_B/s\approx0.4\): the onset of failure. Crowding shrinks \(s\) toward \(\ell_B\).

Why this breaks (B). If \(g=1\), neighbours ignore the ion and only their average matters: (B) is exact. The weight \(e^{\mp\ell_B/s}\) is 0.92 to 1.08 at 1 mM but 0.38 to 2.7 at the electrode (my calculation): like charges avoid the ion and opposite charges crowd it. This correlation hole and pairing tendency is what (B) leaves out.

2 · Why a Growing Surface Roughens

Dobkin and Fraggedakis [2] grow Li from a solid electrolyte at constant current and ask whether a slightly rough, growing surface flattens or roughens (pp.17 to 19).

Split into modes

Schematic (amplitudes illustrative). 1: split the bump into sine modes. 2: each mode only changes its amplitude, by \(e^{\omega(k)t}\), because the flat surface is the same at every \(x\) and the equations are linear for small bumps. 3: add the modes back. Which \(k\) grows is decided by \(\omega(k)\), given by the formula below: wide bumps grow, narrow ones heal (Fig. 7a, p.19).

A mode is one pure wave of the surface: bumps of one width that keep their shape and only change height. For small bumps the modes do not mix, so each one grows or heals on its own, as \(e^{\omega(k)t}\): the extra deposition at a crest is proportional to how far it sticks out, which gives exponential growth.

Field against stress

For wide bumps the paper's result reduces to one formula (Eq. 78, p.20):

$$\omega(k)\simeq\textcolor{#2C6283}{\omega_0+\omega_2k^2}\;-\;\textcolor{#C0473A}{\omega_4k^4}$$

3 · Competing Forces Set a Size

In each case below, one process builds structure and another holds it back, and their balance picks a size. Ball, summing up pattern formation, puts it in one line: “patterns come from a delicate balance between competing forces” ([4] PDF p.159).

Growing electrode. The field brings ions onto crests and grows wide bumps; the stiff electrolyte presses narrow crests, which dissolve toward the valleys. Bumps wider than a critical width grow (section 2, [2] Fig. 7).

The common pattern. One process builds structure, another holds it back. Below, two more cases you can run: a liquid column breaking into beads, and surfactants gathering into micelles.

A thin column of liquid, like the glue on a spider's thread, breaks into evenly spaced beads ([4] PDF pp.67 to 69). Around the column, a thinner neck has a higher pressure and pushes liquid into the bulges, so necks deepen; along the column, the curvature of a short ripple pushes back. Ripples longer than the circumference \(2\pi R\) grow, and the fastest, about \(9R\), sets the bead spacing (textbook result for an ideal column, Rayleigh). The simulation starts from random ripples and grows each one at its Rayleigh rate until the necks pinch; then each piece is drawn as a round bead of the same volume (my code).

Surfactants gather into micelles, or separate

Surfactants in water, schematic.
Add more surfactant to the water.

Schematic, not a simulation. Each molecule is a blue head that likes water joined to a dark tail that avoids it ([4] PDF p.70). Tails gather to hide from water while heads stay outside, and this balance sets a finite size (textbook picture). Ball describes the sequence: alone at low concentration, micelles as it rises, then bilayers that can close into vesicles ([4] PDF p.93). The concentration where micelles first appear is the critical micelle concentration (CMC); to a first approximation, adding more makes more micelles, not bigger ones. If the heads barely prefer the water to the tails, short molecules separate directly into a dense phase with no finite size; longer chains can still form micelles first, even in marginally selective solvents. Panagiotopoulos [3] maps out which one chains with water liking and water avoiding parts choose, depending on solvent selectivity and chain length, with lattice simulations.

Turing's animal patterns share the idea that competing processes set a length, but their mechanism, two chemicals diffusing at different rates, is different (see the animal patterns page).
