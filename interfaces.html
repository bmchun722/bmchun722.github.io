Ions at Interfaces

A short study page in five parts: when mean field electrostatics holds near a charged electrode, what a recent statistical mechanical theory of ion chemical potentials adds, why a growing electrode does not stay flat, how that instability differs from a Turing pattern, and how molecular simulation finds coexisting phases and fits force fields to chemical potentials. Every claim points to an equation or page of the papers listed at the end (page numbers are PDF pages).

1 · When Mean Field Holds

Mean field (Poisson–Boltzmann, Debye–Hückel) electrostatics lets each ion feel only the average potential of all the others. Whether that is allowed is decided by comparing lengths.

Bjerrum length \(\ell_B\)\(\ell_B=\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon k_BT}\) distance at which two unit charges interact with energy \(k_BT\); set by \(\varepsilon\) and \(T\) only[1] Eq. 22, p.10
Debye length \(\lambda_D\)\(\lambda_D=\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon k_BT}{e^2\sum_\gamma z_\gamma^2\rho_\gamma^0}\right)^{1/2}\) radius of the screening cloud around an ion[1] Eq. 21, p.10

Two ratios follow:

$$\varepsilon_c=\frac{\ell_B}{\lambda_D}\ \ \text{(bulk; [1] Sec. IV C, p.21)}, \qquad \Xi=\frac{\ell_B}{\ell_{GC}}=\frac{2\pi\ell_B^2|\sigma|}{e}\ \ \text{(charged wall; [1] p.22)}$$

The paper writes \(\varepsilon=\kappa_D\ell_B\) for the bulk ratio and also uses \(\varepsilon\) for the dielectric constant; here the ratio is called \(\varepsilon_c\) to keep the two apart.

Why a ratio above about 1 breaks mean field

  • Too few ions in the cloud. For a 1:1 salt \(\sum\rho^0=1/(4\pi\ell_B\lambda_D^2)\), so the number of ions inside a sphere of radius \(\lambda_D\) is \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\lambda_D^3\sum\rho^0=1/(3\varepsilon_c)\). An average over the cloud makes sense only if this number is large; at 0.1 M it is already below one.
  • Pair energy above \(k_BT\). Two unit charges a distance \(\lambda_D\) apart interact with energy \(k_BT\,\ell_B/\lambda_D=\varepsilon_c\,k_BT\). Debye–Hückel linearizes the Boltzmann factor, which needs this to be small. [1] (Fig. 3, p.10) puts it as the screening cloud shrinking into the “Bjerrum sphere”.
  • At a wall, discrete ions instead of a smooth layer. The layer is \(\ell_{GC}\approx0.11\) to 0.14 nm thick, while neighbouring counterions sit \(a\approx0.7\) to 0.8 nm apart, about 6 times farther (\(a/\ell_{GC}=\sqrt{2\pi\Xi}\)). A “smeared” charge density is then a poor picture: each counterion sees its neighbours as individual charges. [1] calls \(\Xi\) the electrostatic coupling parameter (p.22). The layer is also thinner than a hydrated ion (radius a few tenths of a nm), so ion size and crowding (term 3 of Eq. 36, Section 2) matter at the wall too, not only correlations.

Assumption: bulk \(\varepsilon=78.4\) everywhere. Water next to an electrode has a lower dielectric constant, which makes \(\ell_B\), and therefore \(\varepsilon_c\) and \(\Xi\), larger still. \(\varepsilon_c\propto c^{1/2}\varepsilon^{-3/2}T^{-3/2}\).

2 · The Ion Chemical Potential

Fraggedakis [1] derives the chemical potential of ion species \(\gamma\) at position \(\mathbf r\) in a primitive model (ions as charged spheres, solvent as a dielectric continuum \(\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\)), Eq. 36 (PDF p.13):

$$\mu_\gamma(\mathbf r)=\underbrace{k_BT\ln\!\big(\Lambda_\gamma^3\rho_\gamma^{(1)}(\mathbf r)\big)}_{1} +\underbrace{V_\gamma(\mathbf r)}_{2}+\underbrace{k_BT\ln\gamma^c_\gamma}_{3} +\underbrace{q_\gamma\langle\phi(\mathbf r)\rangle}_{4} +\underbrace{\tfrac{q_\gamma^2}{2}\!\int\! d\mathbf r'\!\int\! d\mathbf r''\,\Delta_\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')G(\mathbf r',\mathbf r'')\Delta_\gamma(\mathbf r'',\mathbf r)}_{5} +\underbrace{\lim_{\mathbf r'\to\mathbf r}\Big(q_\gamma\!\int_0^1\!d\xi_\gamma\,\psi_\gamma(\mathbf r';\mathbf r,\xi_\gamma)-\tfrac{q_\gamma^2}{2}G(\mathbf r',\mathbf r)\Big)}_{6}$$
  1. Ideal: translational entropy of the ion at local density \(\rho(\mathbf r)\).
  2. External potential \(V\): body forces or ion–wall interactions that are not electrostatic.
  3. Short range activity coefficient \(\gamma^c\): ion size (steric) effects, from the uncharged pair structure \(\hat g^{(0)}\) (Eq. 37).
  4. Mean field electrostatics \(q\langle\phi\rangle\): the applied potential, surface charges and dielectric jumps.
  5. Electrostatic solvation (self energy): the ion’s interaction with its environment through image charges; scales roughly as \((\varepsilon_0\varepsilon)^{-1}\), so it grows where \(\varepsilon\) drops (Born like).
  6. Ion–ion correlations: the energy of the ion in its own screening cloud, through the fluctuation potential \(\psi\); gives Debye–Hückel \(\ln\gamma\) in the dilute limit.

Term meanings from [1], list after Eq. 14 (PDF p.8) and discussion after Eq. 36 (p.13). The picture is a schematic, not from the paper.

What to remember

  • Where it holds. The closures for the pair correlation functions come from a regular perturbation expansion in \(\varepsilon_c=\ell_B/\lambda_D\) (Eqs. 23 to 28, p.10 to 11; Sec. IV C, p.21). The paper states that the model is for ion–ion correlations that are “weak to moderate, as determined by values of \(\varepsilon=\kappa_D\ell_B\)” (p.12). It compares with Grand Canonical Monte Carlo up to \(\varepsilon\simeq5.76\) and agrees, but stresses that “the perturbation is not asymptotically controlled for \(\varepsilon\ge1\)”, so agreement there is empirical (Sec. IV F, p.23): the theory is used beyond its formal range, with caution.
  • Unequal ion sizes. The simple closure breaks the symmetry \(g_{+-}=g_{-+}\) when ions differ in size (Eqs. 29 to 31); a symmetrized closure fixes it (Eq. 35, p.12).
  • Benchmarks are bulk. The demonstration is the primitive model of a valence asymmetric electrolyte with equal ion sizes in the bulk; for symmetric salts it recovers the extended Debye–Hückel result (abstract, p.2). Near a charged wall the paper names \(\Xi\) as a further parameter: “we expect it to play a role” (p.22): the interfacial regime of Section 1 is named as the target, not yet computed.
  • Chemical vs electrochemical potential (Sec. IV G, p.23 to 24). The usual electrochemical potential puts the applied field in \(q\phi\) and lumps ion–ion correlations into the concentration (activity) part. Eq. 36 keeps the applied field contribution (term 4) separate from the correlation contribution (term 6), which the paper argues is clearer near boundaries.
3 · Why a Growing Surface Roughens

Dobkin and Fraggedakis [2] apply their deposition theory to a metal (Li) growing from a solid single ion conductor at constant applied current. Is the flat front stable?

Schematic after [2] Fig. 7b and 7c (PDF p.19). Left: for a wide bump the ion flux goes to the crest, and the valleys dissolve. Right: for a sharp bump the compressive normal stress at the crests raises the chemical potential of the deposited metal there, so the crests strip and pressure gradients in the electrolyte carry ions from crests to valleys. \(k\) is in units of \(1/L\), with \(L\) the electrolyte thickness (millimetres for solid state batteries, p.17).

The recipe: linearize, then one Fourier mode at a time

  1. Base state. Flat front growing at the applied current. The potential in the electrolyte follows from ion conservation, \(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0\Rightarrow e\nabla^2\phi+\Omega\nabla^2p^\beta=0\) (Eq. 61, p.17), which reduces to \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) under plane strain (\(\nabla^2p^\beta=0\)), with the applied current as boundary condition (Eq. 62). The base solution is linear, \(\phi^{(0)}(z)=-Da_i(1-z)\) (Eq. 68, p.18).
  2. Perturb. \(h(x,t)=\langle h(t)\rangle+\epsilon_h\,\delta h(x)\,e^{\omega t}\) (Eq. 70), and expand every field in plane waves, \(f^{(1)}(x,z,t)=\sum_k f^{(1)}_k(z)\,e^{ikx}e^{\omega(k)t}\) (p.18). Each \(k\) gives its own ordinary differential equations in \(z\); in the electrolyte \(\phi^{(1)}_k=\Phi_{1k}e^{kz}+\Phi_{2k}e^{-kz}\).
  3. Why each mode is separate. The flat base state looks the same at every \(x\) (translation invariance), so the linear equations have coefficients independent of \(x\); \(e^{ikx}\) is then an eigenfunction and cannot feed any other \(k\). (Orthogonality is what lets one read off the coefficients afterwards; it is not why the modes decouple.)
  4. The destabilizing term. A crest sits at height \(\delta h\), so the reaction condition is evaluated in the base field at \(z=\delta h\) instead of \(z=0\): the term \(\partial_z\phi^{(0)}\,\delta h_k\) in Eq. 71 (domain perturbation). For long waves this always wins: \(\omega(k\to0)>0\) for any applied current (Eq. 73, p.18).
  5. The stabilizing term. Elastic stress at the interface, \(\sigma^{(1)}_{zz,k}\simeq-\tfrac23\frac{G_o}{1-\nu_\beta}k^4\,\delta h_k\) (Eq. 74), compressive at crests, grows as \(k^4\).

Expanding the full growth rate (Eq. 72) for small \(k\) gives (Eq. 78 = Eq. H.2, PDF p.20 and p.29):

$$\omega(k)\simeq\omega_0+\omega_2k^2-\omega_4k^4,\qquad k_*^2=\frac{\omega_2}{2\omega_4},\qquad \omega_*=\omega_0+\frac{\omega_2^2}{4\omega_4}\ \ \text{(Eq. 79 = H.5)},\qquad k_c^2=\frac{\omega_2+\sqrt{\omega_2^2+4\omega_0\omega_4}}{2\omega_4}$$

with \(\omega_0,\omega_2>0\) for deposition and \(\omega_4>0\) for the elastic parameters considered (p.30). \(\omega_0\) is the field term of Eq. 73; \(\omega_4\) comes from the stress of Eq. 74; \(\omega_2\) comes from expanding \(\tanh(k)/k\) in Eq. H.1 (my reading: the finite electrolyte thickness). Modes with \(k>k_c\) are stabilized by the compressive stress (p.19, p.21); the softer electrolyte (\(G_o=0.1\)) needs a larger \(k_c\) than the stiffer one (\(G_o=2.0\)) (Fig. 7a, p.19). The \(k_c\) formula is the positive root of \(\omega=0\) in this quadratic model, not an equation of the paper, and inherits its \(k^4\) truncation: the zero crossings in Fig. 7a (about 0.4 for \(G_o=2\) and 0.85 to 0.9 for \(G_o=0.1\), read by eye) differ by roughly 20% from this estimate.

Selection is weak here. For the Fig. 7 parameters (\(Da=10\), \(Da_i=0.5\), \(El=20\), \(G_o=2\), \(\nu_\beta=0.33\)), Eqs. H.3 and H.4 give \(\omega_0\approx0.455\), \(\omega_2\approx0.138\), \(\omega_4\approx36\), so \(k_*\approx0.044\) and \(\omega_*-\omega_0\approx1.3\times10^{-4}\) (0.03%). The maximum is a tiny bump on a plateau (Fig. 7a shows no visible peak): growth is nearly flat for small \(k\), so this is a long wave instability rather than a sharply wavelength selected one. The paper still notes the maximum at finite \(k\) (p.20). (My evaluation of the appendix formulas.)

Measured roughness tests the theory directly: each mode grows as \(\delta h_k(t)=\delta h_k(0)e^{\omega(k)t}\), so the structure factor obeys

$$S_h(k,t)=S_h(k,0)\,e^{2\omega(k)t}\ \ \text{(Eq. 75)},\qquad \omega(k)=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\ln\frac{S_h(k,t_2)}{S_h(k,t_1)}\ \ \text{(Eq. 76, p.20)}$$

4 · Three Kinds of Dispersion

The method (linearize, Fourier modes, read the sign of \(\omega(k)\)) is the same in all three; the physics in \(\omega(k)\) differs. For the Turing case see the animal patterns page.

But a Turing instability requires a uniform state that is stable at \(k=0\) and destabilized only by unequal diffusion; in deposition the flat front is unstable already at the longest wavelengths, and stress only cuts off the short ones.
5 · Phase Equilibria by Simulation

The Gibbs ensemble

Two phases coexist when \(T\), \(P\) and every chemical potential \(\mu\) are equal. Simulating a liquid next to its vapour directly needs an interface, which for mixtures meant big systems and long equilibration. Panagiotopoulos’s idea (autobiography [4], p.2 to 3): simulate two separate periodic boxes, with no interface, and let them exchange volume (conjugate to pressure) and particles (conjugate to chemical potential), so that the equilibrium conditions are met implicitly. One run replaces a series of simulations; the Molecular Physics paper (accepted January 1987) is his most cited [4, p.3].

Schematic of the Gibbs ensemble Monte Carlo method as described in [4] (p.2 to 3). Moves of particles inside each box (standard Monte Carlo displacements, not described in [4]) equilibrate each phase internally.

Self assembly: micelles

Surfactants (molecules with a water loving head and a water avoiding tail) stay dissolved as single molecules at low concentration; above the critical micelle concentration (CMC) added surfactant goes into aggregates (micelles). His group obtained CMCs from grand canonical Monte Carlo with histogram reweighting, reading the CMC off the osmotic pressure as a function of concentration [4, p.4]. Why that works (the standard picture, not explained in [4]): the osmotic pressure counts free particles, so its rise slows once new molecules join micelles instead of staying free. A long open question, why simulated systems showed either ordinary phase separation or finite clusters but not both, was traced to solvent selectivity [4, p.4].

Top down force field optimization

Baron and Panagiotopoulos [3] fit classical, polarizable (Drude oscillator) force fields for aqueous salts directly to chemical potential data. The new step is an efficient way to compute derivatives of the salt chemical potential with respect to force field parameters, so gradient based fitting works (abstract, p.2; loss gradient Eq. 23, p.9). None of the models studied (and, to the authors’ knowledge, no current model) reproduces even the slope of the NaCl solid–solution coexistence line (Fig. 1, p.3). At saturation the salt chemical potential in solution equals that of the crystal, so fitting \(\mu\) means fitting solubility.

  • Result. Solubility at one temperature can be improved, but no parameter set could be found that reproduces NaCl solubility at both 298.15 K (6.15 mol/kg) and 373.15 K (6.68 mol/kg): every optimized model overestimates the first and underestimates the second, so the solid–solution coexistence line gets a negative slope, while the measured slope is positive (Fig. 8, p.13).
  • Why. The slope \((dm/dT)_\text{sat}\) depends on the activity coefficients and on the enthalpy of solution (Eq. 35, p.13). The activity coefficient slope at the solubility limit is positive (shown for the Na-CG-2020 models, Fig. 3), so the error is the sign of the model’s enthalpy of solution, which is small but positive experimentally (p.13). In the paper’s words, fixed charge polarizable models cannot describe “the magnitude of the energy change upon solvation relative to the lattice energy” (p.14).
  • Conclusion. Classical polarizability with fixed atomic charges is not enough; charge transfer and many body effects need a first principles description, for example neural network potentials trained on DFT (abstract p.2; conclusions p.14).
