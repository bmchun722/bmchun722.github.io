Ions at Interfaces

Five short notes: when mean field electrostatics holds, the ion chemical potential, why a growing electrode roughens, how that differs from a Turing pattern, and how simulation finds coexisting phases.

1 · When Mean Field Holds

Mean field (Poisson–Boltzmann) electrostatics lets each ion feel only the average potential of the others. It holds when the Bjerrum length \(\ell_B\), where two unit charges interact with \(k_BT\), is the shortest electrostatic length.

$$\ell_B=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon k_BT},\qquad \varepsilon_c=\frac{\ell_B}{\lambda_D}\ \text{(bulk)},\qquad \Xi=\frac{\ell_B}{\ell_{GC}},\ \ \ell_{GC}=\frac{2\varepsilon\varepsilon_0k_BT}{e|\sigma|}\ \text{(charged wall)}$$

Water, \(\varepsilon=78.4\), \(T=298.15\) K, 1:1 salt (my calculation from Eqs. 21 and 22 of [1]).

  • Bulk. \(\varepsilon_c=0.74\) at 0.1 M and 2.4 at 1 M. A Debye sphere then holds \(1/(3\varepsilon_c)<1\) ion, and an ion pair at distance \(\lambda_D\) has energy \(\varepsilon_c k_BT\), so linearized Debye–Hückel fails ([1] Fig. 3, p.10).
  • Wall. At 25 to 33 µC/cm², \(\Xi\approx5\) to 6.6: the counterion layer (0.11 to 0.14 nm) is about six times thinner than the spacing between counterions (0.7 to 0.8 nm), and thinner than a hydrated ion, so discreteness and ion size both matter. (\(\ell_{GC}\) and \(\Xi\): [1] p.22; the surface charges are my illustration of typical values.)
2 · The Ion Chemical Potential

Fraggedakis [1] writes the chemical potential of an ion in a nonuniform electrolyte as six terms (Eq. 36, p.13, written compactly; meanings p.8):

$$\mu_\gamma(\mathbf r)=\underbrace{k_BT\ln(\Lambda^3\rho)}_{1\ \text{ideal}}+\underbrace{V}_{2\ \text{wall}} +\underbrace{k_BT\ln\gamma^c}_{3\ \text{ion size}}+\underbrace{q\langle\phi\rangle}_{4\ \text{mean field}} +\underbrace{\mu^{\text{self}}}_{5\ \text{solvation}}+\underbrace{\mu^{\text{corr}}}_{6\ \text{correlation}}$$
  • Terms 4 and 6 are kept apart: applied field vs ion–ion correlations, which the usual electrochemical potential lumps into the activity (Sec. IV G, p.23 to 24). Term 5 scales as \((\varepsilon_0\varepsilon)^{-1}\), so it grows as \(\varepsilon\) decreases, for example with added salt (p.13).
  • The closures come from a perturbation expansion in \(\varepsilon_c\); the theory is for “weak to moderate” correlations (p.12). It matches GCMC up to \(\varepsilon_c\simeq5.76\), but the expansion “is not asymptotically controlled for \(\varepsilon\ge1\)” (the paper's \(\varepsilon\) is \(\varepsilon_c\) here) (p.23): beyond that, use with caution.
  • Benchmarks are bulk electrolytes; at a charged wall, of \(\Xi\) the paper says “we expect it to play a role” (p.22).
3 · Why a Growing Surface Roughens

Dobkin and Fraggedakis [2] grow Li from a solid single ion conductor at constant current and perturb the flat front with modes \(\delta h_k e^{ikx}e^{\omega(k)t}\) (p.18). Each \(k\) evolves on its own because the flat state is the same at every \(x\) (translation invariance). The potential obeys ion conservation, \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) under plane strain (Eq. 61), with the applied current as boundary condition (Eq. 62).

The equation chain

$$h(x,t)=\langle h(t)\rangle+\epsilon_h\,\delta h(x)\,e^{\omega t},\qquad f^{(1)}(x,z,t)=\sum_k f^{(1)}_k(z)\,e^{ikx}e^{\omega(k)t}$$

Unknown here: how a small bump of any shape evolves.

Solved: nothing yet. The bump is written as a sum of plane waves; the flat state is the same at every \(x\), so each \(k\) evolves on its own (Eq. 70 and the expansion below it, p.18).

Hands on: for each \(k\), ordinary differential equations in \(z\) only.

$$C_{ijkl}\,u_{kl,j}=0\ \ (\text{Eq. 63}),\qquad u^{\beta}_{zk}(0)=u^{\alpha}_{zk}(0)+\delta h_k\ \ (\text{Eq. G.6})$$

Unknown here: displacements \(u_{xk}(z),u_{zk}(z)\) in metal and electrolyte.

Solved: first, without the potential (one way coupling). In my reading of Eq. G.6, the metal pushes the bonded electrolyte up by \(\delta h_k\); the electrolyte top is free (Eq. G.4).

Hands on: the normal stress at the interface \(\sigma_{zz,k}(0)\) (App. G, pp.28 to 29). For small \(k\): \(\sigma_{zz,k}\simeq-\tfrac23 G_o k^4\delta h_k/(1-\nu_\beta)\) (Eq. 74).

$$\nabla^2\phi=0\ \ (\text{Eq. 61 with }\nabla^2p^\beta=0)\ \Rightarrow\ \phi^{(1)}_k=\Phi_{1k}e^{kz}+\Phi_{2k}e^{-kz}$$ $$-\partial_z\phi^{(1)}_k-\Omega_0El\,\partial_z p^{\beta(1)}_k=-Da\sqrt{1+\tfrac{Da_i^2}{4Da^2}}\Big[\phi^{(1)}_k+El\big(\Omega_0p^{\beta(1)}_k+\sigma^{(1)}_{zz,k}\big)+\partial_z\phi^{(0)}\,\delta h_k\Big]\ \ (\text{Eq. 71})$$

Unknown here: the two constants \(\Phi_{1k},\Phi_{2k}\).

Solved: from the counter electrode condition (Eq. 62) and the reaction condition at the interface (Eq. 71).

Hands on: the perturbed deposition current. Eq. 71 holds both rivals: the field term \(\partial_z\phi^{(0)}\delta h_k\) (more ions to the crest) and the stress term \(El\,\sigma_{zz,k}\) (adding metal at a compressed crest costs more).

$$\omega(k)=\frac{\sqrt{4Da^2+Da_i^2}\,\big(Da_i+El\,\hat\sigma_{zz,k}(0)\big)}{2+\sqrt{4Da^2+Da_i^2}\,\tanh(k)/k}\ \ (\text{Eq. 72}),\qquad \hat\sigma_{zz,k}=\sigma_{zz,k}/\delta h_k$$

Unknown here: \(\omega(k)\), the answer.

Read it: in the numerator, \(Da_i\) (field) is the same for every \(k\) and grows bumps; \(El\,\hat\sigma_{zz}\) (stress) is negative and its size grows with \(k\). The denominator is positive, so it does not move \(k_c\); it rescales \(\omega\) and shifts the peak slightly. \(\omega(k_c)=0\) where they cancel.

Limits: \(k\to0\): \(\omega\simeq Da_i\sqrt{4Da^2+Da_i^2}/(2+\sqrt{4Da^2+Da_i^2})>0\) (Eq. 73), field wins. Large \(k\): stress wins.

After [2] Fig. 7b, c (p.19). Left (\(k<k_c\)): ions flow to the crest and the valleys fall behind. Right (\(k>k_c\)): compressive stress raises the metal’s chemical potential at crests, so ions move from crests to valleys.

The destabilizing term is the base field acting on the displaced surface, \(\partial_z\phi^{(0)}\delta h_k\) (Eq. 71), which gives \(\omega(k\to0)>0\) (Eq. 73). Stress \(\sigma_{zz}\propto-k^4\delta h_k\) stabilizes short waves (Eq. 74). For small \(k\) (Eqs. 78, 79; H.2, H.5):

$$\omega(k)\simeq\omega_0+\omega_2k^2-\omega_4k^4,\qquad k_*^2=\frac{\omega_2}{2\omega_4},\qquad k_c^2=\frac{\omega_2+\sqrt{\omega_2^2+4\omega_0\omega_4}}{2\omega_4}$$

\(k_c\) is my root of this \(k^4\) truncation; it differs from the Fig. 7a crossings by about 20%. Selection is weak here: for the Fig. 7 parameters \(\omega_*-\omega_0\approx10^{-4}\) (my evaluation of Eqs. H.3, H.4), so growth is a near plateau up to \(k_c\), a long wave instability rather than a sharply selected wavelength. Each mode grows as \(S_h(k,t)=S_h(k,0)e^{2\omega t}\) (Eq. 75).

5 · Phase Equilibria by Simulation

Gibbs ensemble. Coexisting phases need equal \(T\), \(P\) and \(\mu\). Panagiotopoulos simulated two periodic boxes with no interface that exchange volume and particles, so these conditions hold implicitly in one run ([4] p.2 to 3).

Micelles. Above the critical micelle concentration (CMC), added surfactant forms aggregates; his group read the CMC from the osmotic pressure versus concentration computed by grand canonical Monte Carlo ([4] p.4). In the standard picture (not from [4]), the osmotic pressure counts free particles, so its rise slows at the CMC.

Top down force fields. Baron and Panagiotopoulos [3] fit polarizable force fields directly to salt chemical potentials using parameter derivatives of \(\mu\) (abstract, p.2; Eq. 23). For NaCl no parameter set could be found that matches solubility at both 298 and 373 K: the coexistence line slope keeps the wrong sign, traced to the enthalpy of solution (Fig. 8, Eq. 35, p.13), so fixed charge polarizability is not enough (p.14); no existing model gets the slope (Fig. 1, p.3).
