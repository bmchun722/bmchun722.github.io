Where Does the Barrier Come From?

In coupled ion-electron transfer (CIET), an electron can only jump once the surrounding solvent has rearranged and the ion has moved. This animation shows the two free-energy surfaces of the reaction and how the transition state shifts between a solvent-controlled and an ion-controlled picture.

The ratio \(s = \Delta E_{IT}/\lambda\) sweeps from 0.1 to 10 and back on a log scale while \(\lambda + \Delta E_{IT}\) is held fixed. The surfaces follow eq. (6) of Fraggedakis et al. (2021); the quadratic form of the ion-transfer term is an illustrative choice.

The Two Surfaces

Marcus theory describes electron transfer with one coordinate \(x\), the polarization of the surrounding solvent. CIET adds a second coordinate \(\xi\), the distance the ion moves. Each redox state then has its own bowl-shaped free-energy surface:

$$\mu_O(x,\xi) = \mu_O^0 + \frac{\kappa}{2}(x - x_O)^2 + f_O(\xi), \qquad \mu_R(x,\xi) = \mu_R^0 + \frac{\kappa}{2}(x - x_R)^2 + f_R(\xi)$$

The paper constrains the ion-transfer terms \(f_O, f_R\) but does not fix their form: each must vanish at its own equilibrium point, and moving the ion without the electron (or vice versa) costs the ion-electron separation energy \(\Delta E_{IT}\). The simplest functions with those properties, and with a minimum at each equilibrium point, are parabolas in \(\xi\) (taking \(\xi_O = 0\)):

$$f_O(\xi) = \Delta E_{IT}\,\frac{\xi^2}{\xi_R^2}, \qquad f_R(\xi) = \Delta E_{IT}\,\frac{(\xi - \xi_R)^2}{\xi_R^2}$$

The electron can tunnel only where both states have the same energy, the orange line \(\mu_O = \mu_R\). The reaction most likely crosses at the lowest point of that line, the yellow saddle point.

What the Animation Shows
  • Small \(s\) (\(\lambda \gg \Delta E_{IT}\)): the bowls are steep along \(x\) and shallow along \(\xi\). The cost of crossing comes from reorganizing the solvent, as in classical Marcus electron transfer.
  • Large \(s\) (\(\Delta E_{IT} \gg \lambda\)): the bowls are steep along \(\xi\). The cost of crossing comes from moving the ion.
  • The iso-energetic line rotates between the two limits, and the saddle point stays midway between the two minima because the driving force is zero.
Limits of this picture. With quadratic ion-transfer terms and \(\lambda + \Delta E_{IT}\) fixed, the saddle-point height is the same, \((\lambda + \Delta E_{IT})/4\), for every \(s\). The animation therefore shows where the barrier comes from, not how fast the reaction is. The different rate laws of the two CIET limits (Marcus-like versus Butler-Volmer-like kinetics) come from integrating over the electron energies in the electrode, which a free-energy surface alone does not show.
Two Limits of CIET

CIET is the general theory. Which transfer limits the rate decides which familiar rate law it reduces to [2, 4].

Marcus kinetics and Butler-Volmer kinetics are therefore not rival models inside CIET; they are its two ends.

Why HER Is Treated as ICET

Yoon and Bazant apply CIET to the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) in acid on many metals [4]. They do not derive which limit HER falls in. They choose ICET from experiment and then check that choice against data.

  1. Observation. HER in acid shows linear Tafel plots over a wide range of overpotential, as Tafel first reported. Marcus theory cannot explain this, which Bockris called the “Achilles’ heel” of the quantum theory of electrode reactions.
  2. Theory. Within CIET, only the ion transfer limited regime (ICET) gives the Butler-Volmer equation. The electron transfer limited regime (ECIT) gives MHC kinetics.
  3. Choice. The ICET result applies “whenever BV kinetics and Tafel’s Law are observed”, and the HER analysis is carried out “assuming the ICET reaction mechanism”.
  4. Check. ICET predicts that \(\log_{10} j_0\) is linear in the electrode work function \(\Phi\), with slope \((1-\alpha)/(k_BT\ln 10)\). Trasatti’s HER data give a slope of 6.6 per eV, so \(\alpha \approx 0.61\). Metals fall on two parallel lines. A constant shift of the surface potential at the reaction plane, \(\Delta\chi_r = -0.4\) V for the negatively charged sp metals, collapses them onto one line (\(R^2 = 0.98\)). In the ECIT regime the paper expects a dependence that differs significantly from this simple Arrhenius form.
A consistency check, not a proof. The linear Trasatti plot agrees with ICET; it does not rule out every alternative. The same paper suggests that CO2 reduction may instead be limited by electron transfer, that is, by ECIT.
Weak and Strong Electronic Coupling

Electron transfer connects two diabatic states: \(|\psi_D\rangle\), with the electron on the donor, and \(|\psi_A\rangle\), with the electron on the acceptor. In reduction at an electrode, the donor is the metal and the acceptor is the reactant. The two states mix through the electronic coupling matrix element [4]

$$H_{DA} = \left|\langle \psi_A | \hat V_{DA} | \psi_D \rangle\right|$$

At a metal the donor is a continuum of levels. The two-state picture below treats one metal level; in the rate expressions the metal enters through the chemisorption function \(\Delta_e = \pi H_{DA}^2 \rho_e\) [4]. Along a nuclear coordinate \(x\), with phases chosen so that the coupling is real and positive, the two-state Hamiltonian in the diabatic basis and its eigenvalues, the adiabatic surfaces, are

$$\hat H(x) = \begin{pmatrix} E_D(x) & H_{DA} \\ H_{DA} & E_A(x) \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_\pm(x) = \frac{E_D + E_A}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{E_D - E_A}{2}\right)^2 + H_{DA}^2}$$

Where the diabatic surfaces cross (\(E_D = E_A\)), the adiabatic surfaces are split by \(2H_{DA}\). Assume that \(H_{DA}\) does not depend on \(x\) (Condon approximation) and that the diabatic surfaces are parabolas of equal curvature at zero driving force. The crossing point then drops by \(H_{DA}\), and the barrier on the lower surface becomes \(\lambda_\text{eff}/4\) instead of \(\lambda/4\) [4, 5]:

$$\lambda_\text{eff} = \lambda\left(1 - \frac{2H_{DA}}{\lambda}\right)^2, \qquad 2H_{DA} < \lambda$$

What This Means for Electrocatalysis

In electrocatalysis the electron goes to a species that binds to the metal surface, or becomes bound by the transfer itself, as H does in the Volmer step. The coupling \(H_{DA}\) can then be large, and the transfer may well be adiabatic. The rigorous CIET derivation, however, is non-adiabatic: it treats the reaction as a transition between distinct charge localized states and integrates the golden rule rate over the Fermi sea of the metal [4].

Yoon and Bazant bridge this gap in two ways [4]:

  • ICET. A rigorous derivation with adiabatic electron transfer is left for future work. The paper uses a transition state theory prefactor instead and argues that, although the prefactor changes form, the shift of the activation free energy by \((1-\alpha)(\Delta\varepsilon_F - e\Delta\chi_r)\) does not depend on the degree of adiabaticity. In the paper’s argument, this shift follows from the modified electron concentration \(\tilde c_e\) (Eq. 12 of [4]).
  • ECIT. Following Abraham et al. [5], the golden rule prefactor is replaced by one with \(k_BT/h\), and \(\lambda\) by \(\lambda_\text{eff}\), which keeps the MHC form of the rate.
In short. Electrocatalytic charge transfer may well be adiabatic, while the theory is derived for the non-adiabatic case. For the Butler-Volmer limit (ICET), the paper argues that the work function and surface potential dependence is unchanged, while the prefactor changes form. For the Marcus limit (ECIT), adiabaticity changes both the prefactor (golden rule to \(k_BT/h\)) and the barrier (\(\lambda\) to \(\lambda_\text{eff}\)).
