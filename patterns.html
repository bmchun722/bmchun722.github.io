The Model

Turing (1952) showed that two reacting, diffusing chemicals can turn a uniform mixture into a stationary pattern. Gierer and Meinhardt (1972) identified the ingredients: a short-range activator that promotes its own production, and a long-range inhibitor that diffuses faster. James Murray proposed in the late 1970s that such a pre-pattern in the embryonic skin decides where melanocytes make pigment. His coat-pattern calculations use the substrate-inhibition kinetics of Thomas (1975), written in dimensionless form as

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \gamma\, f(u,v) + \nabla^2 u, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} = \gamma\, g(u,v) + d\, \nabla^2 v,$$ $$f(u,v) = a - u - h(u,v), \qquad g(u,v) = \alpha\,(b - v) - h(u,v), \qquad h(u,v) = \frac{\rho\, u\, v}{1 + u + K u^2}.$$

Here \(u\) and \(v\) are the two morphogen concentrations (in Murray's account of Thomas's enzyme experiment, a substrate and a co-substrate: uric acid and oxygen). Both are supplied at constant rates (\(a\), \(\alpha b\)) and consumed together by the reaction \(h\). Because \(h\) saturates and then falls for large \(u\) (the \(K u^2\) term), the kinetics act as an activator-inhibitor pair with a built-in ceiling on self-enhancement. That is the kind of constraint Ball says stripes typically need (footnote, p. 166). The simulator solves the equivalent system with lengths rescaled by \(\sqrt{\gamma}\) and time by \(\gamma\) (\(x' = \sqrt{\gamma}\,x,\ t' = \gamma t\)), so that \(\gamma\) appears only as the domain size \(L = \sqrt{\gamma}\); the times shown are \(t'\).

Which Parameters Give Which Coat

Linearising about the uniform steady state \((u_s, v_s) \approx (9.93, 9.29)\) gives the Jacobian \(f_u = 2.52,\ f_v = -8.83,\ g_u = 3.52,\ g_v = -10.33\). Since \(f_u > 0\), \(u\) is self-enhancing (the activator); \(g_v < 0\) and \(f_v < 0\) make \(v\) the inhibitor. A small perturbation \(\propto e^{i k x}\) grows if \(\det\!\big(J - k^2\,\mathrm{diag}(1, d)\big) < 0\), which first becomes possible when \(d\) exceeds $$d_c \approx 9.63, \qquad \lambda_c = 2\pi/k_c \approx 7.4 \ \text{(in units of } L/\sqrt{\gamma}\text{)}.$$ Linear theory only says whether a pattern forms and its wavelength: in 2-D every wave direction with \(|k|\) in the unstable band grows equally fast, so it cannot choose between stripes and spots. That choice is made by the nonlinear terms. The stripe/spot ranges below are therefore observed in runs of this simulator (several random starts) on the default body (\(\gamma = 3600\), \(u^* = 12\)); the transition is gradual, and the boundaries are rough and specific to these settings.

CoatParametersReason
Plain (no instability) \(d < d_c \approx 9.6\), any \(\gamma\). The inhibitor does not outrun the activator. Every perturbation decays back to the uniform state, so the skin is one colour everywhere. Diffusion only smooths; there is no Turing instability.
Plain (too small) \(d > d_c\) but \(L = \sqrt{\gamma} < \pi / k_{\max}\): no mode fits for \(\gamma \lesssim 5\) (\(d = 30\)) or \(\gamma \lesssim 7\) (\(d = 12\)). Modes just inside the band edge grow very slowly, so in practice the domain stays plain up to \(\gamma \approx 6\)–\(9\). The instability exists, but no allowed mode fits in the domain: with zero-flux edges the wavenumbers are \(k = \pi\sqrt{(m/L_x)^2 + (n/L_y)^2}\), and even the longest one lies outside the unstable band. Murray's explanation for uniformly coloured small mammals (and for small embryos).
Stripes (zebra preset) \(d\) just above \(d_c\): roughly \(9.6 < d \lesssim 13\). Preset: \(d = 12\), \(\gamma = 3600\). Near onset only a narrow band of wavelengths around \(\lambda_c\) can grow, and growth is slow. In these runs the nonlinear terms then select stripes (long, connected dark bands). The saturation of self-enhancement (\(K u^2\)) is the kind of constraint Ball notes stripes typically need.
Mixed roughly \(13 \lesssim d \lesssim 18\). Stripes and spots coexist; stripes break into chains of spots. The change is gradual.
Spots (leopard preset) \(d\) well above \(d_c\): roughly \(d \gtrsim 18\). Preset: \(d = 30\), \(\gamma = 3600\). The fast inhibitor confines each activator peak to a compact island. In these runs isolated, round peaks are what forms, so the pre-pattern becomes an array of spots of high \(u\).
Spots → bands (cheetah tail preset) Spot-forming \(d = 30\) on a tapering tail. Where the circumference is shorter than the shortest unstable wavelength \(2\pi/k_{\max}\) (\(\approx 4.3\) for \(d = 30\)), nothing can vary around the tail, so only rings fit (bands). Where the tail is wide, spots fit. This is Murray's model-tail result (Ball, Fig. 4.7); Ball notes that real tails, e.g. of cheetahs and leopards, are often spotted at the root and banded at the tip (Fig. 4.6).
Numerical Method

Method of lines: the Laplacian is a second-order 5-point finite difference on a grid with spacing \(h \approx 0.7\) (about ten cells per wavelength), and time is advanced with explicit forward Euler, $$\Delta t = \min\!\left(0.9\,\frac{h^2}{4d},\ 0.02\right),$$ where the first bound is the diffusion stability limit and the second keeps the stiff reaction term stable. The body uses zero-flux (Neumann) edges via reflected ghost cells. The tail is a cone cut open along its length, so it is periodic around the circumference and zero-flux at both ends; each grid row has as many cells as its circumference holds, and neighbouring rows are coupled by nearest position. This ignores the small curvature term of the cone's Laplacian, which is acceptable for the gentle taper used here. The simulation stops at \(t = 400\) to save CPU: by then the pattern has mostly formed, but it keeps rearranging slowly (moving \(d\) or pressing Resume runs it further). Very small domains need tiny time steps, so for them the run is cut off earlier by a compute budget. Source: assets/murray-patterns.js.
